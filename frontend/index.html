Accuracy check vs NASA maps

Our simulation is compared with NASA's own long-term sunlight and Earth-visibility maps (18.6-year averages) at sites published inside the Artemis III regions, and at random points near the pole. NASA's maps are the reference, so this tests our geometry and terrain handling, not NASA's data.

How to read this

The idea. NASA already published maps of how often every spot near the pole is sunlit and can see Earth, averaged over the Moon's 18.6-year cycle. If our own simulation is right, it should reproduce those numbers from scratch using only the terrain and the positions of the Sun and Earth.

The table. For each published Artemis-region site: NASA's value, our value, and the difference in percentage points (Δ). A difference near 0 means we agree. Green is within 2 points, cyan is within the tolerance, red is beyond it.

The random-point line. The same comparison at 60 random spots near the pole, so one lucky site cannot flatter the result. Average error shows a bias (+ means we are too bright). Typical error (RMSE) is how far off we usually are. Within 3 pts is the share of spots that agree closely. Correlation is 1.00 when our map has the same pattern as NASA's.

What it proves and does not. It shows our geometry and terrain handling are sound. NASA's map is treated as the truth, so it cannot show NASA's own errors. Large errors on steep ground are expected, because the terrain data is coarser than the slopes.

The last column. The authors who proposed these sites claimed they were sunlit and Earth-visible enough. We check those claims against NASA's own map: "misses" means NASA's map does not support the claim at that exact spot.
